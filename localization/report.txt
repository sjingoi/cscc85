Q1) What is the total number of probabilities your robot has to keep
track of for a map of size m x n?

The total number of probabilities our robot has to keep track of for a map of size m × n is 4 × (m × n).
This is because each location on the map can have four possible orientations (north, south, east, and west), 
and the robot maintains a probability for each direction.

Q2) What should be the initial value of the probabilities your robot is
keeping track of?

The initial probabilities are uniformly distributed because the robot initially has no knowledge of its exact position.
Therefore, each location–direction pair is assigned an equal probability of 1 / (4 × m × n), reflecting complete uncertainty.

Q3) Describe your strategy for dealing with sensor noise, and
explain why it should work and how you tested it.

Our strategy for handling sensor noise involves training the color sensor using a set of reference points representing all expected colors.
When detecting a color, the robot compares the current HSV reading to the stored training data and selects the closest match using a nearest-neighbor 
approach. This method works well because it reduces the impact of small fluctuations in sensor readings. We tested it by repeatedly scanning known 
colors under different lighting conditions and verifying consistent classification accuracy.

Q4) Describe your probability update function in detail and
explain how you defined 'agreement' as well as how you
Determined the amount by which to update probabilities.

Our probability update function adjusts the belief of each possible location–direction pair based on whether the robot’s sensor observations agree with the map data.
“Agreement” is defined as the correspondence between the expected color at a given location and the actual color detected by the sensor. When the observation agrees
with the map, the corresponding probability is increased proportionally; when it disagrees, it is decreased. The magnitude of the update is determined using a Bayesian 
update rule, normalizing all probabilities afterward so that they sum to one. This ensures the robot’s belief remains consistent and probabilistically sound.


Q5) Describe in detail your robot’s exploration strategy – it should
be more complex than simply moving in the same direction
until hitting the boundary of the map. How did you choose this
Strategy?

Our robot’s exploration strategy begins by attempting to locate an edge of the map, which provides a fixed reference point. Once it reaches an edge, it proceeds in a 
straight line, scanning intersections and updating its belief about its position as it moves. When it hits another boundary, it turns and continues in a new direction, 
systematically covering the map. We chose this strategy because it provides a balance between coverage and efficiency, ensuring the robot gathers enough unique sensor 
readings for reliable localization.

Q6) Describe in detail how beliefs are shifted when the robot gets
to a boundary. Consider this problem carefully, the correct
way to handle this is not obvious. Explain clearly what
assumptions regarding the robot's motion support your
strategy for shifting belief values in this case.

When the robot hits a boundary, it reverses to the previous intersection, updates its beliefs to reflect the boundary constraint, and then rotates 90° to the 
left before continuing. This approach assumes the robot’s motion is reliable and that it can accurately detect when it reaches the boundary. By returning to the 
previous intersection, the robot avoids accumulating localization errors that could occur if it attempted to continue from an invalid position beyond the boundary.

Q7) Describe in detail how beliefs are shifted when the robot turns
90 degrees (either left or right) without going to a different
Intersection. Describe your assumptions about robot motion,
and how these support your strategy for handling this case.

When the robot turns 90 degrees at an intersection, it records the new orientation but does not immediately update its positional belief.
Instead, it waits until it has moved to the next intersection to apply the update, ensuring that belief changes are based on confirmed motion and observations.
This assumption relies on consistent turning behavior, allowing the robot to maintain an accurate internal representation of its direction before updating its 
overall probability distribution.

Q8) Explain your method for deciding when the robot has achieved
correct localization.

We determine that the robot has achieved correct localization when one particular location–direction pair’s probability becomes significantly higher than all 
others, typically around 50% or greater. At this point, the robot has accumulated enough consistent sensor and motion data to confidently identify its 
position on the map.

Q9) Briefly explain your robot design choices, including sensor placement
and robot mobility considerations.

Our robot uses rubber treads instead of standard wheels to minimize slippage and rotational drift. A small front-mounted weight was added to balance the 
robot’s initially rear-heavy design, improving its rotational accuracy. The color sensor is mounted low and near the front to maximize color detection 
accuracy, while the gyro sensor is placed at the robot’s center to ensure stable and consistent rotation measurements. Together, these design choices 
enhance both motion precision and sensor reliability.

Q10) What parameters did you find affect the most the ability of the robot to
determine its location?

The most critical factor affecting localization accuracy was sensor noise, particularly from the gyro sensor.
This sensor was essential for maintaining correct orientation between intersections, but frequent drift and calibration errors often caused inaccurate turns and readings.
These issues introduced uncertainty into the localization process and required frequent recalibration.

Q11) Can external factors (illumination in the room, for example) affect the
ability of the robot to find itself?

Yes, environmental illumination has a significant effect on the robot’s ability to detect colors accurately.
Because the color sensor was calibrated under specific lighting conditions, changes in brightness or light source color can cause the detected HSV values to deviate from the training data.
This discrepancy can lead to misclassification and hinder accurate localization.

Q12) On average, how many intersections does your robot need to visit
to achieve successful localization?

Based on experimental testing, the robot typically achieves successful localization after visiting three to five intersections.
At that point, enough sensor data has been gathered to distinguish between similar map locations and converge on a confident belief distribution.

Q13) Are there locations in the map that make localization harder? (i.e.
does it matter where your robot starts its journey? Why?)

We found that initial location does not significantly affect localization difficulty.
Our exploration and scanning strategy allows the robot to gather sufficient information regardless of its starting point.
Since it systematically explores edges and intersections, it can localize itself effectively from any initial position.
