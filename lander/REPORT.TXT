1) What was the impact of having noisy sensors in your ability to
   determine the state of the craft and ultimately control it 
   properly?

   Noisy sensors caused multiple issues including instability with landing,
   inability to create alternate readings due to noise accumulation, and innacurate
   state estimation. 

   Instability with landing: Noisy sensor readings caused oscillations and overshooting
   early into the project, as some of our preliminary code responded too quickly to changes.

   Inability to create alternate readings: When primary sensors failed, our secondary AND
   tertiary readings were initially inaccurate since noise from other sensors would be combined
   and thus amplified.

   Inaccurate state estimation: Position and velocity noise made it hard to accurately determine
   trajectory and proximity to the landing zone. This ultimately affected navigating precision and
   landing timing.

2) Describe the technique(s) you used to deal with sensor noise

   We looked into the linear filtering and convolution method from lecture, applying the convolution
   with 3 different filters being Gaussian, Triangular, and Uniform. These 3 would be used for Velocity,
   Position, and Sonar respectively as they all have their own benefits. Gaussian's bell curve shape provides
   smooth stable readings but is less responsive to sudden changes. Triangular shape allows for balance between
   smoothing and responsiveness. Finally, Uniform shape filters out noise spikes.

3) Describe your strategy for dealing with thruster failures

   We decided to base our thruster logic off the usage of one thruster at a time, relying on rotational control, 
   combined with whatever thrusters are still functional. When side thrusters fail, we rely on main thruster and 
   when main thruster fails we rely on a side thruster. This structure allows for control over the lander even with
   significant thruster failures.

4) Describe your strategy for dealing with sensor malfunctions:

   - Position sensors
   - Velocity sensors
   - Angle
   - Sonar

   What if you have multiple failures? what could you do if, for
   example both position and velocity sensors became inoperative?

   Our general strategy for dealing with sensor malfunctions is to have a total of 3 ways to get the reading. 
   The first is the standard, read from sensor and denoise, the second is to use alternative working sensors 
   if the primary one is non-functional, and the third is to use a combination of range finder + any other 
   working sensors. Using These 3 readings, we can get alternate readings for any sensor that breaks,
   including our position and velocity sensors being inoperational.

5) What was the hardest failure type to deal with (i.e. which is
   the component, either sensor or control that makes the lander
   harder to handle?)?

   Position sensor failure is what we had the most trouble with, as deriving the alternate readings using velocity
   and using just range finder is very hard. Position is fundamental to navigation and landing precision, the craft 
   struggles to determine its location relative to the landing site without it. Integrating velocity to estimate 
   position accumulates errors over time, while using range finder with angle requires calculations that are sensitive 
   to angle sensor noise. Additionally, position sensor failure affects velocity derivation when velocity sensors also 
   fail, creating a compounding problem that makes the craft harder to control reliably.

6) Assume a human pilot was in charge of controlling the craft 
   (i.e. the pilot has control of thrusters and rotation).
   How would you change Safety_Override() so that it prevents
   the pilot from accidentally crashing the lander?

   We would modify Safety_Override() to implement a multi-layered safety system that monitors critical parameters and 
   intervenes when needed. The system would continuously check horizontal position, velocity, and obstacle proximity using our 
   denoised sensor readings. If the craft descends too rapidly (vertical velocity exceeding safe thresholds) or approaches 
   the ground at dangerous speeds, the override would automatically engage main thrusters to reduce descent rate regardless 
   of pilot input. For obstacle avoidance, the system would monitor sonar readings and temporarily take control to steer away 
   from detected hazards, then return control once clear. Additionally, we would implement velocity limiting that prevents 
   the pilot from exceeding safe speed limits in any direction, and landing zone protection that prevents commands directing 
   the craft toward unsafe terrain. In extreme cases where pilot actions consistently threaten the craft, the system 
   could engage full autopilot mode to execute an emergency landing sequence, prioritizing crew survival, using the same 
   sensor fusion techniques we developed for normal operation.
   
7) Do you think it is reasonable to write such a control function?
   (i.e. a routine that takes control from a human for their
    own safety)

    Yes, we think that it is reasonable to write such a control function for a craft like this as human error can be
    devastating for the mission. Compared to vehicles like cars where drivers can recover from their mistakes, any
    mistakes made on a lander liek this in space result in loss of the craft or loss of the entire onboard crew. On 
    top of this, leaving the complexity of managing various onboard controls and readings to a human pilot is just
    very risky, and therefore the usage of an override feature would be beneficial. It is good to not completely take
    away control from the human pilot during safety override though, as the crew should still be able to prevent fatal
    errors possibly made by the function.

8) Should the pilot be aware that this software is present?
   (yes or no, and tell me why you think so!)

   Yes, we think it is important that the human pilot is aware that there is such software on the craft. This software
   is important as it potentially stops many human errors from completely failing the mission, but it is equally important
   that all crew knows it exists. This way, the pilot knows that there is something that will commence in such scenarios, and
   therefore will not be surprised by it. Additionally, pilot awareness enables proper training on when and how the system
   intervenes, allowing them to work cooperatively with the safety override rather than fighting against it.

9) Would you buy/drive a car that you know has control software
   that will take control from you if it estimates you are
   doing something dangerous?

   We think it depends on how often and in what scenarios does the software take over. If the system only intervenes in genuinely 
   life-threatening situations (like preventing collisions or loss of control), then yes, we would be comfortable with it 
   since the consequences of accidents are severe. However, if the software frequently overrides normal driving decisions or has 
   a high rate of false positives, it could become frustrating and potentially dangerous if drivers become overly reliant on it. 
   The key difference from the lander scenario is that car driving involves many subjective decisions and varying road conditions 
   where human judgment is often superior to automated systems. We would want the car's safety override to be limited to clear 
   emergency situations with well-defined parameters, similar to existing features like automatic emergency braking, rather than 
   general driving assistance that could interfere with normal operation.
   
10) Can you as an embedded systems designer think of every 
    possible scenario that could go wrong so as to write 
    control software that ALWAYS works?

    Think about that...

    No, it is impossible for us to anticipate EVERY possible scenario that could go wrong. Even with comprehensive sensor fallback
    system and thruster failure handling, there are just infinite edge cases and combinations that we haven't considered. the complexity 
    of interactions between multiple simultaneous failures grows exponentially, making it impossible to test every combination. 
    Real-world systems must be designed with graceful degradation in mind, accepting that perfect reliability is unachievable. Instead, 
    we focus on robust design principles: redundancy, fail-safe defaults, comprehensive testing of likely scenarios, and emergency protocols 
    that prioritize the most critical objectives (like preventing catastrophic failure) when facing unknown situations. The goal isn't 
    perfection, but rather building systems resilient enough to handle the vast majority of realistic failure modes while maintaining basic 
    safety even in unprecedented circumstances.
